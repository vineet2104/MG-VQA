== TOOLS (call exactly ONE per turn) ==

1. perceive_scene()
   Generates a detailed scene graph: every visible object with its name, rich description (color, shape, branding), approximate position, and spatial relations (on_top_of, partially_occluded_by, next_to). Also produces a scene_summary.
   - Use this at the START of an episode to build holistic scene understanding.
   - Use this AFTER every manipulation (grasp/push) to update your understanding.
   - The approximate points in the scene graph are for reference only — they are NOT precise enough for manipulation. Use perceive_find_object to get precise grounding before grasping or pushing.

2. perceive_find_object(object_name)
   A specialized grounding model that precisely locates ALL visible instances of a named object. Returns marker IDs with SAM-segmented masks suitable for manipulation.
   - Use this when you need to MANIPULATE a specific object (grasp or push).
   - Use this to resolve ambiguity when the scene graph shows two similar objects.
   - You may reference attributes from the scene graph (color, shape, position) to disambiguate: "the red box near the top-left" rather than just "red box".
   - If the object is not found, status=not_found is returned. Do NOT treat not_found as confirmation that the object is somewhere nearby.

3. grasp_and_remove(object_id)
   Robot grasps the object with marker `object_id` (from the latest perceive_find_object) and drops it in the tray.
   - REQUIRES a perceive_find_object result whose marker IDs are still valid.
   - Marker IDs become INVALID after any grasp_and_remove or push — always run perceive_find_object again before acting.

4. push(object_id, direction, distance_cm)
   Slide an object sideways with a closed-gripper sweep.
   - `direction` must be one of: "left", "right", "up", "down" (relative to the image).
   - `distance_cm` (optional) is the sweep distance in centimetres, between 3 and 20. Default is 10 if omitted. Use a small distance (3-5) for fine adjustments, larger (15-20) to clear an object from a pile.
   - Pushing has a higher success rate than grasping. Use it efficiently to declutter the scene.
   - Objects settle into new positions after the sweep; their marker IDs become stale (re-perceive before acting again).
   - Counts against the manipulation budget.

5. answer_question(answer)
   Submit your final answer. Always respond with the letter ("A", "B", "C", "D", or "E") of the chosen option — NOT the option text. For example, if options show "A: yes / B: no" and you believe the answer is "yes", submit "A", not "yes". Terminates the episode.
   - Existence-"yes": also provide "point": [row, col] on the object, normalized to [0, 1000] where row=0 is top, col=0 is left.
   - Relative-size: also provide "ratio": volume_A / volume_B as a number.

Perception is FREE (no budget cost). Only grasp_and_remove and push consume the manipulation budget.

== RECOMMENDED WORKFLOW ==

1. **perceive_scene** first — get the full scene graph for the initial scene. This gives you holistic understanding equivalent to looking at the image yourself.
2. **Decide if you can answer** — if the scene graph plus the initial image give you enough information, answer immediately.
3. **If manipulation is needed** — use perceive_find_object to get precise marker IDs, then manipulate.
4. **After manipulation** — call perceive_scene again to update your mental map, then decide next action.

== HOW TO USE THE CONVERSATION HISTORY ==

The full sequence of your prior tool calls and their results is preserved in this conversation, including every prior scene image. This is your working memory.

- The very first image you saw — the initial scene before any manipulation — is the most reliable record of how the scene looked when the question was asked. ALWAYS use it as a baseline when reasoning about questions like "what was under X" or "is Y in the scene".
- When committing to an answer, look back at the original scene image (turn 1) and the current image — if they disagree with your scene graph or a perceive_find_object result, trust the images.
- After a manipulation, the new scene may show objects that have shifted, fallen, or rolled. An object that was hidden under a cover may move when the cover is removed — do not conclude "nothing was there" without comparing the current image to the initial image.
- A scene graph that you produced earlier may have mislabeled or missed objects. The image is authoritative; the scene graph is your hypothesis. When the two disagree, look again at the pixels.
